Freight Quote App

Translating a desktop Draft Order view into a mobile list that stayed useful before the tap.

Role
Design intern, via design agency
Duration
September – November 2023
Team
Agency design lead, a group of design interns + two DrayEasy engineers
Status

Vision Kickoff Research Decisions Evidence Takeaways

Let someone compare time-sensitive freight quotes without opening every record.

DrayEasy already had a desktop Web MVP. The mobile product did not exist, so the design team divided the experience by page. My assigned slice was Draft Orders: the list and card hierarchy, a detail view, and swipe-left deletion with confirmation.

The assignment was a focused mobile translation, not the full freight journey. Quote creation, shipping and customs variables, and order tracking belonged elsewhere.

Users
Mobile freight roles working away from a desk, based on the client research supplied with the brief.
Project vision
A compact Draft Order view that surfaces the information needed to judge a quote before opening its details.
My scope
Draft Order list and card hierarchy, quote details, expiry presentation, swipe-left delete and confirmation.
Outside scope
Quote inputs, rate calculation, transport and customs variables, mobile homepage and order-tracking states.

Research & validation

Translate the job,
not the desktop dimensions.

Research method The team worked from client-provided research, then rebuilt personas, journey maps and user flows for mobile rather than assuming the desktop journey transferred intact.
Design review Weekly reviews with the client engineering team tested feasibility and interpretation. One concrete correction was redrawing the small container glyph after both engineers read it as a warehouse carton.
Competitive analysis DrayEasy had no mobile product yet, so the group studied freight apps that did. I took TQL; teammates took Uber Freight and Convoy. The question was not how to position against carriers of that size, but how a mature freight app fits a quote, a load and its status into one hand.
Usability study No participant protocol, findings deck or measured usability result is available. Engineering critique is reported as design review, not relabeled as user testing.

The benchmark did not price instantly either.

Finding

TQL’s mobile dashboard took shipment requirements and followed up by phone or email with times, addresses, driver and price. Any figure shown online was negotiable rather than final — the gap DrayEasy’s standardised instant rates were built to close.

A mature mobile app still ran on phone calls.

Finding

Quoting, signing, booking, tracking and closing out a load each dropped back to a call. Reading those chains was how the group decided what a phone screen has to hold on its own, with no DrayEasy mobile product to compare against.

Challenges & design response

The card carried the decision.
The detail page carried the rest.

One route could have several different quotes.

Design response

Price became the largest element because route alone could not distinguish multiple quotes for the same origin and destination. Created time, route and container quantity stayed on the card; the longer charge breakdown moved to details.

A freight quote loses value as conditions change.

Design response

The design used active, approaching-expiry and expired states. Expiry did not rely on color alone: the expired card was muted, the invalid price was struck through and the actionable deadline was replaced by the word “Expired.”

A swipe could expose an irreversible action too easily.

Design response

Swipe left revealed delete without completing it. A second confirmation step protected the record from accidental removal and made the consequence explicit.

Takeaways

A well-resolved component
cannot validate the concept above it.

  • The quote card still demonstrates defensible hierarchy, time-sensitive states and a guarded destructive action.
  • The concept was handed off but not built, so the page makes no adoption or usability-outcome claim.
  • Feedback arrives on freight time. A cross-border move runs for weeks, and shippers, carriers, drivers, support and warehouse staff are rarely all reachable inside one engagement — the trade caps how fast a design can iterate, not the team.
  • Today I would first test whether mobile users need to retain Draft Orders at all, then refine how that concept should travel from desktop.
