Architecture

A single-page React app with no database. Everything it knows is either fetched on demand or recomputed at render time.

Full stack breakdown
Frontend
React 18 with Vite, a single Zustand store with selective persistence, Tailwind over a CSS-variable design system, and Lightweight Charts. No router.
Analysis
About 2,900 lines of dependency-free analysis logic turn normalized market and company evidence into horizon-specific readings.
Backend
Fifteen endpoints on the serverless handler signature, fronting eight upstream sources across keyed providers and public data services. API keys live in the server environment only, while endpoint-level fallbacks choose the available source — the browser never calls a vendor directly.
Storage
No database. Server-side caching dies with the process; client-side there are three independent localStorage layers with their own versioning. Nothing computed is ever persisted.
Resilience
Abort timeouts, provider cooldowns after rate limiting, graceful degradation that returns an empty shape rather than an error, and headers naming the data source and the reason a series came back empty.
Demo mode
Three hand-authored scenarios that short-circuit the network entirely, with a test asserting each scenario’s final candle close equals its quoted price.

Design

Most dips are just support

The failure this was built against is a personal one: buying loud. Chasing a name while the market is shouting about it, or — worse — taking a profit at a high and putting it straight back in without stopping to look at the trend, the volume, or what the disclosures show. The money handed back that way is usually money that had already been made.

Buying the dip has stopped working as a strategy on its own. Most of what looks like a dip is a support level inside a longer trend rather than a bottom, and the two look identical on the chart — the difference only appears in the data around the price. So that distinction is the entire job of this product: enough analysis, and enough history behind the move, to say whether this particular dip is one worth taking.

One user, and that user is me. It has never been shown to anyone else, and nothing on this page should be read as validation by someone who was not also the author.

Similarity without an outcome is astrology

Historical similarity is usually hand-waving. Here it is a defined operation: returns are z-normalised, compared by cosine similarity over a sliding window, de-duplicated so overlapping windows do not double count — and each match records what actually happened afterwards. Similarity without forward return is astrology; with it, it is a sample.

A zone as wide as the data deserves

The entry zone is anchored at the 25th percentile of swing lows, shifted by a detected trend regime, blended with recent lows — and then its width is clamped by a multiple derived from volatility, so a calm high-priced name gets a tight zone and a volatile one gets a wide one.

Twelve negative articles, still only cautious

Sentiment is weighted by recency and relevance — a regulatory filing scores zero — then deduplicated, and each publisher’s contribution is capped so no single prolific feed can manufacture a signal. A negative verdict additionally requires two independent sources.

The current test suite asserts this design intent directly: twelve negative articles from one publisher still resolve to merely cautious. It also covers deterministic demo cases, source caps and deduplication, SEC separation, and weak-signal guards for insider and congressional activity.

Selected Detail

A scraper for a site with no API

Congressional trade disclosures come from a government portal with no API. The client bootstraps a session, extracts a token, accepts the usage agreement, keeps a hand-rolled cookie jar, then fetches filing pages five at a time and parses the HTML. On failure it caches an empty result for an hour so a broken upstream is never hammered.

Challenges

A note on evidence. This repository has eleven commits, each a large squashed batch, with a four-month gap in the middle. The outcomes below are well documented but the deliberation is not — where I cannot show the thinking, I say so.

01

Building too much, then cutting three times

A personal tool with no external pressure to choose will accumulate features forever. It took three passes and about 7,500 deleted lines to get to one question.

Read the full write-up
Step 01Problem

The project opened at about 5,300 lines and four weeks later took on another 7,161 — learning modules, a trade planner, a personality profiler, a glossary, a news feed, price prediction.

Nothing was broken. It simply had no centre, because “it is only for me” makes every addition feel free.

Step 02What I tried

First, a retreat from prediction to risk. The similar-patterns tab became historical risk assessment — win rate, worst case, median return. The algorithm survived; what changed was what it claimed.

Second, a redesign that deleted the non-core modules outright: 72 files, +10,608 / −6,179.

Each of those failed for a reason that only shows up in use, not on a feature list. Learning modules and the glossary — nobody opens a trading tool to be taught what a 50-day moving average is, and someone who needs that explanation is not about to act on it either. The personality profiler — knowing your own temperament does not tell you whether this dip is real, because a US-market dip turns on the index, on breaking news and on earnings far more than on the disposition of the person watching it. The trade planner — a buy sequence takes minutes to write and the setup can be gone in seconds; putting one on screen implies a durability the market does not offer, and the call belongs to whoever is carrying the risk. The general market-news feed — market-wide headlines are browsing material. What replaced it is per-ticker news read alongside SEC filings, which is the only news that bears on one specific decision.

Third, another subtraction pass six weeks later, listing more removals than additions — a strategy radar, a scale-in ladder, a heatmap, a snapshot card, an entire evidence-snapshot subsystem. The scale-in ladder had been added twenty-four days earlier. One round of focusing had not been enough.

Step 03The fix

The product definition collapsed to a single sentence. Everything retained has to feed that one call — technicals, drawdowns, fundamentals, insider activity, macro and news are inputs to one verdict rather than six separate tabs.

Architecturally that became a layered decision that two independent veto stages can downgrade. The output is not a number but a ranked evidence list plus a short set of conditions that would have to change.

Step 04Outcome

About 7,500 lines removed. Not quantified: there is no usage data behind any of it — the user base is one person. It also had a side effect worth admitting: the documentation kept describing deleted components, so it advertised a product that no longer existed. That has since been rewritten.

  • Personal tools lose focus fastest, because nothing external forces a trade-off.
  • One focusing pass is rarely enough.
  • Deleting code means deleting the documentation that describes it.
  • Narrowing what a feature claims can beat improving what it computes.
02

A working prototype that never became a feature

The rule engine, the modal, the translations and the trigger all exist. None of them was ever connected. I now treat it as an abandoned experiment, not a launch decision recorded at the time.

Read the full write-up
Step 01Problem

Buying into a fall is where people talk themselves into things. So I built a devil’s advocate: before committing, generate the strongest arguments against the trade — twelve rules across three severity tiers.

The trigger is the sharp part: it fires when historical matches are highly similar but historically unprofitable — precisely the setup where familiarity is most likely to be mistaken for edge.

Step 02What I tried

The rule engine works. The modal exists, with focus trapping and translations in both languages. The trigger fires and writes to the store.

None of the four pieces was ever connected to the others. I checked this across every commit rather than assuming: the rule engine has never been called from anywhere. It was not broken by a later refactor — it was never wired at all.

Step 03What I would do now

My later judgement, informed by day-trading but not documented in a contemporaneous commit, is that the feature does not survive its intended execution timing.

During fast intraday moves, price can shift while a user reads the objections, checks the original entry, opens a broker, finds the ticker and places an order. The modal adds delay precisely where its advice is most time-sensitive.

For a long-horizon holder it is unnecessary anyway — the fundamentals and drawdown history already on the page answer the same question without an interstitial. Neither audience is served.

This is retrospective reasoning, not proof that the unfinished integration was an intentional product decision when the code was written.

Step 04Outcome

The experiment did not become part of the working flow. I present it as an abandoned prototype and keep the useful lesson separate from claims about why it stopped.

It is also consistent with where the product went. The last pass removed the duplicate risk cards, the snapshot subsystem, and the numeric composite score — every one of them a layer between the user and the call. A blocking modal is that same shape.

  • A feature can be correct in isolation and still fail against the timing of its actual use.
  • A later explanation should not be presented as a decision recorded at the time.
  • Abandoned code is evidence of exploration, not evidence of a deliberate launch choice.
  • Precision should match input quality.
